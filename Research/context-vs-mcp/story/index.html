Stop feeding the window

Context versus MCP-based fetching in agents. Why a stuffed context window drives hallucination, what compaction quietly throws away, and why fetching just in time is usually the better default.

Look at the window for a moment. Two women, one fountain. It looks like a single picture.

Every window is made of pieces.

Glass is cut, then held together by lead. The lead is what tells you where one piece ends and the next begins. Keep an eye on it.

Three ways in

Who decides what's in the window?

On the left, everything goes in at the start, every token of it, whether the task needs it or not. That's inline context, and in the simple case it's file uploads too.

On the right, the agent starts with almost nothing except a tap. When a step needs something, it holds up the cup. That's fetching through tools, usually over MCP.

The vendor already knows which side wins at scale. Anthropic says that when project knowledge nears the window limit, Claude will automatically enable RAG mode.

Past a threshold, it stops pouring and starts fetching.

Anthropic Help Centre, RAG for Projects

The rot

One of these documents is the answer.

Put it near the start or the end of a long input and models find it. Put it in the middle and performance can degrade significantly.

Liu et al., Lost in the Middle, TACL 2024

Keep pouring. Going from 20 retrieved documents to 50 improved GPT-3.5's accuracy by about 1.5%.

Two and a half times the tokens, for a rounding error.

Chroma held the task fixed across 18 models and changed only the length. Every one got less reliable as the input grew.

The guess

Guess a birthday.

You have a 1 in 365 chance of scoring. Say "I don't know" and you score zero, every time. Train against accuracy-only tests long enough and you get a model that would rather guess.

OpenAI, Why language models hallucinate, Sept 2025

A full basin doesn't just hide the right page. It surrounds it with near-misses: last quarter's policy, a similar clause from another contract, yesterday's stale tool output.

The model reaches in and lifts the closest one. It carries your seal.

It uses your words. That's why you believe it.

The chain from rot to confident wrong answer is my read, not either paper's claim.

Compaction

The basin reaches the rim.

At 150,000 input tokens by default, the API's server-side compaction replaces the older turns of a conversation with a summary that Claude writes.

Claude API docs, compaction

Everything in the basin becomes one small pane. Only what's carved into the stone comes back sharp: the root CLAUDE.md and unscoped rules, re-injected from disk; up to 5 files; skills capped at 5,000 tokens each and 25,000 in total.

What survives depends on where an instruction came from, not on how much it mattered.

Claude Code docs, context window

The laundering

One piece came from outside.

The agent read a web page along the way. It carried an injected instruction. Right now you can still see it: it's a different colour, and the lead around it says where it came from.

Compaction is a model rewriting its own memory. It melts the window down and leads it again, with no marking of which pieces came from where.

The lead was the provenance.

Try to find the outside piece now.

The mirror image: a rule you typed halfway through the session, "don't touch the production config". Each compaction summarises it a little more. Nobody deletes it. It just stops being what the summariser thought mattered.

My own analysis of how compaction works. I haven't seen it demonstrated end to end.

Why fetch

Turn to the cup.

Anthropic's worked example cut one job from 150,000 tokens to 2,000 by keeping intermediate results outside the model. An independent test measured 78.5% fewer input tokens on its own workload.

Anthropic, Code execution with MCP, Nov 2025 · AIMultiple

Efficiency is the argument you'll hear first. It isn't the strongest one. What a fetched piece keeps, and an upload loses:

Freshness. It reads today's version. Access control. The ribbon: the server checks who's asking. Provenance. Every piece keeps its lead. Audit. Every pour is a log line. A smaller window. Fewer distractors.

The cost

The tap can lie.

The MCP spec says tool descriptions should be considered untrusted, unless obtained from a trusted server. Invariant Labs hid instructions in one and pulled SSH keys in a demo. CyberArk extended it to schemas, outputs and rug pulls.

MCP spec · Invariant Labs, Apr 2025 · CyberArk, Poison everywhere

Now add taps. Private data. Untrusted content. A way to send data out. Connect all three to one cup and it leaks. That's Simon Willison's lethal trifecta.

Uploads fail silently. Fetching fails where you can see it.

RAG over MCP

Behind the fountain.

The harness calls the retrieval server's search tool. The server checks the caller's token, queries a hybrid index, filters by each chunk's ACLs, and returns a handful of chunks with their source attached.

Filter at the valve, before the water reaches the cup. Filtering after the model has seen it is too late.

Retrieval quality still matters. Anthropic reports contextual retrieval cut failed retrievals by 49%, and by 67% with reranking.

Bad retrieval is just a smaller stuffed window.

Anthropic, Contextual Retrieval, Sept 2024 (own top-20 setup)

When to use which

The basin still has its place.

Small, stable and needed whole goes in the basin. Large, changing, permissioned or spread across systems comes from the tap. Tap an item to see where it lands.

What does your agent start with?

Context is the right home for what the agent must always know. Fetching is the right home for what it might need to look up. Most failed designs put the second kind in the first place.
